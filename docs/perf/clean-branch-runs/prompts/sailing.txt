Fixing a position at sea with a sextant depends on measuring the angle between a celestial body and the visible horizon at a precisely known instant, then comparing that measured altitude with the altitude the body would have if the observer stood at an assumed position nearby. The difference between the two, expressed in minutes of arc, converts directly to nautical miles, because one minute of arc along a great circle on the Earth's surface is by definition one nautical mile. The navigator therefore does not compute latitude and longitude from a single sight. Instead the sight yields a line of position: a short segment of a circle on which the observer must lie, drawn perpendicular to the bearing of the body and offset from the assumed position by the computed difference.

The measurement itself has three corrections that matter more than the arithmetic that follows. The index error is the instrument's own bias, found by bringing the horizon into coincidence with itself through the mirrors and reading the residual angle on the arc. Dip accounts for the observer's height above the water, since a raised eye sees a horizon that lies below the true horizontal plane, and the correction grows with the square root of the height in metres. Refraction bends light from the body toward the observer as it passes through denser air near the surface, so every observed altitude is slightly too large, and the correction becomes severe below about ten degrees, which is why experienced navigators avoid low sights unless nothing else is available.

Timing is the other half of the problem. The Earth turns through fifteen degrees of longitude every hour, so an error of four seconds in the recorded time of the sight displaces the resulting line of position by one minute of longitude, which near the equator is a full nautical mile. The chronometer therefore carries as much responsibility as the sextant, and its rate of gain or loss is logged daily so the navigator can apply a correction rather than trust the dial. A watch set to a radio time signal serves the same purpose today, but the discipline of recording the instant of contact between the body's limb and the horizon has not changed.

Two lines of position that cross at a good angle produce a fix. Morning and evening twilight are the productive windows, because the horizon is still visible while the brighter stars and planets have appeared, and a round of three or four bodies spread around the compass gives lines that intersect in a small triangle rather than a long sliver. A noon sight of the sun is the exception that gives latitude alone: at meridian passage the sun's altitude stops changing, and the latitude follows from the observed maximum altitude and the sun's declination for the day, with no timing required beyond knowing roughly when the passage will occur.

The advance of a single line by dead reckoning is what lets a sun sight taken in the morning combine with another taken in the afternoon. The navigator moves the earlier line along the course steered by the distance run, then crosses it with the later one.
