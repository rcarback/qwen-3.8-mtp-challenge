Indigo differs from almost every other natural dye in that the colouring compound is insoluble in water and must be chemically reduced before it will enter a fibre. The blue pigment, indigotin, is converted in the vat to a pale yellow-green form called leuco-indigo by removing oxygen from the molecule, and only that reduced form dissolves and penetrates cotton or wool. When the wetted cloth is lifted from the vat and exposed to air, oxygen returns to the compound within the fibre and the blue reappears, first as a greenish tint at the surface and then as a deepening blue as the reaction proceeds inward. The dyer therefore builds colour by repeated short dips and airings rather than by a single long immersion, because each cycle deposits another layer of pigment that has already been trapped in place.

The reduction can be achieved in several ways, and the choice shapes the whole working rhythm of the vat. A fermentation vat relies on bacteria that consume a sugar source such as fruit or bran and strip oxygen from the bath as a by-product, which takes days to establish and must be fed and kept warm to stay active. A chemical vat using sodium hydrosulfite acts within an hour but exhausts itself quickly and needs fresh reducing agent at every session. Between them sits the iron vat, in which ferrous sulfate and lime produce the reducing conditions slowly enough to remain stable for weeks, at the cost of an alkaline bath that is harsh on wool and silk.

Alkalinity is the second condition that must be held, because leuco-indigo dissolves only in a basic solution. Lime, soda ash, or wood-ash lye raise the pH, and the dyer watches for the coppery sheen on the surface of a healthy vat and the characteristic yellow-green colour of the liquid beneath it. A vat that has turned blue throughout has taken up too much oxygen, usually from stirring or from cloth being dropped in rather than lowered, and it must rest or be fed before it will dye evenly again. Excess alkali, on the other hand, damages protein fibres and produces a harsh hand, so wool is dyed at a lower pH and a lower temperature than cotton even though both take the same pigment.

Depth of shade is controlled by the concentration of dissolved pigment and by the number of dips, not by the length of any single dip. A pale sky blue may need only two brief immersions in a weak vat, while a navy that will resist crocking may take twenty or more in a strong one, with a full airing of several minutes between each. Cloth that is dipped too long simply sits in the bath without gaining colour, because the pigment can only be fixed at the moment of oxidation.
